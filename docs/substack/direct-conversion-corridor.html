The direct-conversion corridor starts from a cost premise: converting a larger fraction of fusion power into net electricity could reduce the fusion power, core size and fuel required for a given electrical output. Following Six Candidate Corridors to 1¢/kWh Fusion, this study examines a plant that relies on direct energy capture as its primary route to electricity.

That choice quickly brings us to deuterium-helium-3, or D-He3. D-T releases about 80% of its fusion energy as neutrons. The primary p-B11 reaction produces charged particles, but in the plasma regimes examined in our earlier direct energy conversion analysis, much of the power leaves as bremsstrahlung. D-He3 combines charged primary reaction products with lower radiative losses, leaving the largest share available for direct capture among the fuel cases compared there. Its helium-3 supply and the neutrons and tritium produced by D-D side reactions remain substantial constraints.

The fuel choice leaves the confinement and conversion architecture open. A pulsed magnetic-compression field-reversed configuration, or FRC, offers a specific reason to investigate it: the coil circuit that compresses the plasma can also recover energy as the plasma expands. The same system must recover much of the driver energy and capture additional fusion energy. Its efficiency, cost and component lifetime therefore directly affect the plant’s net output and electricity cost.

Helion’s published architecture, patents and prototype work provide a concrete basis for examining this arrangement. We use that public record to estimate the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) for a pulsed inductive D-He3 plant at four evidence grades. This tests one implementation of the direct-conversion premise. Other confinement and recovery arrangements remain to be assessed. Helion’s FAQ has projected its cost of electricity production at $0.01 per kWh ($10/MWh) “without assuming any economies of scale from mass production, carbon credits, or government incentives” [1], and that figure is the 1cFE target.

The findings, in brief. Four devices share the architecture and differ only in the evidence grade of what they are granted: citable records alone, Helion’s own requirements at face value, labeled extrapolations, and speculation. They read 112.1, 105.9, 25.7 and 10.5 $/MWh at 1 GWe, and a 3 GWe park of three speculation modules reads 8.2 $/MWh, so the cheapest coherent machine misses the 1 cent/kWh target as a 1 GWe plant and clears it as a park, on a 3% real cost of capital worth 6.1 $/MWh there, on a 40 T compression field that no public source names and whose coil this work does not price, and on a driver bank four times Polaris’s.

Every number grants Helion’s shot: a gain of 2.56 on the bank, the ratio that turns Polaris’s 50 MJ into the 50 MWe Helion has contracted to supply Microsoft, firing at 1 Hz, scaled with the bank at the grades that enlarge it. Nothing in the record fixes it, so without the grant there is no machine to cost; every figure here rests on it, the records device’s included.

The fuel cycle, not the driver, is the load-bearing subsystem, because He-3 has no fleet-scale market: what trades does so by the gram at 19-22 M$/kg, against the 2 M$/kg this study charges. The optimized devices therefore breed most of their own and buy the rest at a price nothing supports.

The fuel choice starts from its direct-conversion potential. Within that fuel system, the cost optimization favors a deuterium-rich mixture that leaves a smaller fraction of fusion energy available for direct capture but reduces purchased He-3 through internal breeding. That trade adds radiation, neutron and tritium burdens, which must be included in the plant cost.

The costing uses the 1costingfe bottom-up cost model [2], organized by cost account (the CAS codes in the tables below) and built on publicly available data and labeled extrapolations; the open-source code is available here, with detailed documentation here, and the commit is pinned under Reproducibility. Three of its behaviors carry the results: the driver is priced per joule of stored energy and its whole shot-cycled recovery chain (driver bank, recovery store, switch stack) is replaced at the capacitor shot life; the conversion account is priced on the energy the physics path actually returns; and the tritium store’s He-3 return is credited against purchases through a study-side scaling of the He-3 price (Section 5.1).

This work is organized as follows: Section 2 describes Helion’s device from the published material, Section 3 defines the four devices and the constraints they are optimized under, and Section 4 reports their cost. Section 5 works out the He-3 fuel cycle, and Section 6 bounds what a shot can return and what the circuit recovers of it, which is where Helion’s shot parts from Helion’s stated pulse. Section 7 asks what would reach $10/MWh and what stands in the way, and Section 8 concludes.

2. Machine basis

The machine follows Helion’s published architecture at face value [3, 4]. Two FRC plasmoids, formed by theta-pinch field reversal in opposed formation sections, are accelerated and merged in a central compression section, the collision converting their directed kinetic energy into ion heat, and magnetic compression then does the adiabatic work to burn conditions. The pulsed-power driver is the only heater. After the burn the plasma expands against the field, returning driver energy and charged fusion energy to the coil circuit as work; radiated power, undirected charged power, and neutrons are not converted.

The design-basis shot is built from three public numbers: the driver delivers 50 MJ per pulse (the sourced Polaris capacitor bank), the plant fires at 1 Hz, and Orion, the first plant, is to supply Microsoft at least 50 MW, which Helion describes as at least 50 MWe after an initial ramp-up period [3]. The yield per shot is whatever makes the model turn that shot into 50 MWe at 1 Hz at the burn point Helion’s patent discloses: 128.1 MJ, a gain (fusion yield over delivered driver energy) of 2.56. The gain is a consistency figure, not a disclosed measurement.

Helion’s stated product is the 50 MWe module: its CEO describes a gigafactory with 50 MW generators coming off the line and leaving on a truck, one a day [5]. No public source says how many such modules make up Orion; this work takes it as one, as that framing implies, and the solved yield scales inversely with the count. The chamber radius is 0.61 m, the smallest at which a 10 m cylinder wall survives the shot under the tungsten flash bound of 1.5 MJ/m^2 per pulse; the Helion device at 1 GWe is 20 modules at 1 Hz. In general a plant is an integer number of Helion-class modules, with the repetition rate and module count solved jointly from the net-power target under a repetition-rate ceiling.

That ceiling, the compression field and the bank are design choices in this work: a plant may fire slower in more modules, the burn point is the patent’s at the field a device compresses to, and the bank is a count of modular capacitor units. The next section grades all three by evidence.

The public record as of September 2026 is D-T fusion on Polaris at 150 million degrees C. Helion has published no yield or recovered energy, and has withdrawn the term “net electricity” from its Polaris goals [3, 6]. Helion does not report the data inputs a cost analysis needs, and this work fills each gap with graded evidence tiers (Section 3): the yield of a burning pulse and its burn time; the energy recovered with plasma present, which the FAQ says has been measured on seven prototypes and gives no figure for; the compression density and field of the reactor point; the per-pulse separation rate and the per-pulse fuel recovery of the exhaust loop; and the duty cycle at which a machine fires for hours. The capacitor cost at cycle life, the other datum the ladder rests on, is a supplier’s, not Helion’s.

3. Method

Notation. 1costingfe model parameters keep their configuration-file names. dd_frac (dhe3_dd_frac) is the D-D share of primary fusion events by count, not an ion ratio: the patent’s He3/D = 0.158 at 70 keV is dd_frac 0.49, Helion’s mix; the 3:1 D:He-3 mix is 0.31; the D-rich mix is 0.85. dhe3_f_T is the fraction of bred tritium that burns in-pulse. eta_dec is the circuit efficiency of inductive recovery, f_pdv the fraction of the plasma’s expansion work that reaches the coils, and their product the composite recovery. chi is the plasma-thermal share of the delivered driver energy and x_s the separatrix-to-coil radius ratio; the other parameters are named where they enter.

3.1 Design search and reconciliation

Each device except Helion’s own configuration is the minimum-LCOE design of a scan over the compression field up to its grade’s grant, the driver bank, the shot, chamber length and radius, wall class, seven burn mixes each sitting on its bremsstrahlung floor, and the firing rate, discarding any design that violates one of the coherence constraints below. The shot is pinned to the design-basis gain of 2.56 except at speculation, where the scan chooses the gain.

Each device’s firing-rate ceiling is the lowest of its grade’s grant, the rate at which one shot’s burn would fill the pulse period, and its wall’s flux bound. The rate is scanned down from there in steps, each forcing more and slower modules.

Each device is quoted at its minimum and bracketed by a reconciliation band. The band’s top adds the two costs the model names but cannot price internally, the high-voltage DC switch duty and the driver-thermalization derate; its bottom credits the one conservatism, no thermal bottoming. The tritium plant, carried as a range rather than a point, moves both ends.

3.2 The field and the bank, graded

Neither is pinned at its sourced value, because those values are an experiment’s rather than a plant’s: Polaris’s bank, and the patent’s burn point, which is the highest field in the record. Both are scanned instead, and of the two the field is where nearly all of the saving comes from.

The field is a stepped entry of the ladder: 22 T at the design basis and records, the field the patent’s operating point (0.75e22 m^-3 at 70 keV) implies at beta = 1, corroborated by Helion’s stated compression field of more than 15 T; 30 T at extrapolations, with no public roadmap behind it; and 40 T at speculation, an explicit leap. The upper two are grants rather than records. One step above each grade’s field, 30, 40 and 50 T, is priced as a labeled sensitivity excluded from that grade’s devices.

The bank is held at Polaris’s 50 MJ at the records grade, doubles to 100 MJ at extrapolations and reaches 200 MJ at speculation. Doubling it is a procurement rather than a technology, since a bank is a count of the modular capacitor units Helion is tooling its Omega facility to produce for Orion, about 2,500 of them [7].

The burn point follows the field. At beta = 1 and the patent’s ion temperature the density scales as B^2, and the rest follows: the breakeven time, the burn a plasma needs to return its own energy, falls as B^-2; the per-pulse burnup and the magnetic pressure rise as B^2; the compressed flux volume E_del/(B^2/2mu0) falls as B^-2 and grows with the bank. The coils are priced at the field and the driver per joule, and the wall’s steady flux and per-shot flash follow the fusion power and the shot.

3.3 Evidence ladder

The plant environment is held in part and stepped in part. Every entry is engineering except the cost of capital, which is financing and is reported separately:

- Held at every grade: constant-dollar framing, and EPCM indirects at 20% of direct cost [8], which the model scales with the build time (CAS30 = fraction x CAS20 x (construction_time_yr / 6 yr)), so the effective fraction is 16.7% at a 5-year build, 10.8% at 3.25 years and 8.3% at 2.5 years.

- Construction time, stepped: 5 years at the design basis and records grades (the serial Hualong One builds, about 60 months from first concrete to grid [9]), 3.25 years at extrapolations (the Kashiwazaki-Kariwa ABWR pair, 37 and 39 months from first concrete to fuel load [10], at the peak of a mature light-water supply chain), 2.5 years at speculation (a combined-cycle gas turbine timeline for a fusion module with a tritium facility).

- Cost of capital, stepped: 7% real at the design basis and records grades (the IEA/NEA central case [11]), 5% at extrapolations (a loan-guarantee blend with precedent), 3% at speculation (the IEA/NEA low case [11], sovereign-class capital).

- Book life, stepped: 60 years through the records grade, a series-consistency entry rather than a record, adopted because the companion studies hold 60 at their records grade. It sits below the licensed bound of 80 years [12] and above the fusion literature’s 30-40 year cluster [13]. 80 years from the extrapolation grade (the US DOE hydropower lifespan [14]). Every core component is replaced on its own schedule and the unreplaceable structure sees negligible fluence.

- Availability, derived: 0.98 outside planned outages, less a routine outage every two years [15], a capacitor-bank swap at the bank’s shot life, and a wall-and-coil changeout at the displacement-damage tolerance (dpa, displacements per atom; 20 dpa through the records grade, 50 above it): 0.893 at the records device and 0.925 at speculation, against the fusion literature’s 0.75 to 0.90.

The performance entries step the same way. Two are requirements Helion’s design implies rather than publishes: per-pulse fuel recovery, which the He-3 economy needs to close, held at 0.999 on the records device by the JET fuel-retention analogy with its ITER-like wall [16] and at four nines on the Helion device; and the shot, which has no value to revert to, so every device is granted it. The cheaper stacks keep both grants and add more, the largest being capacitor life 1e9 shots [17]. Every entry carries a source and grade in the evidence ledger (Markdown), and no extrapolation entry is a measured record.

3.4 Coherence constraints

Eight couplings are carried on every quoted point. Constraints 1, 2, 3, 5 and 8 are enforced by the scripts as checks; 4, 6 and 7 are applied by construction:

1. The radiated fraction is a function of the burn mix through a bremsstrahlung floor, because a deuterium-rich plasma radiates more of its fusion power, not less: 0.28 at dd_frac 0.85 against 0.20 at Helion’s 0.49. Helion’s own patent figure, 0.23, sits above its floor, so the Helion device is priced at the patent’s value. Independent reconstructions put Helion’s fusion-to-bremsstrahlung ratio 3.6 to 9 times [18] or about an order of magnitude [19] too favorable, which would put the radiated fraction well above the floor.

2. Recovery efficiency is capped as the composite eta_dec x f_pdv (0.80 through the extrapolation grade, 0.90 at speculation), because the only published figure speaks to the product.

3. The wall must survive two loads. Its steady flux must stay within the limit its wall class is qualified to, what the coolant carries away continuously; and its per-shot flash, deposited in about a millisecond before conduction can spread it, must stay under the tungsten melt bound of 1.5 MJ/m^2, taken with no margin. Two classes are purchasable: a panel wall of the ITER first-wall kind, qualified to about 2 MW/m^2 at 0.35 M$/m^2, and a divertor-class monoblock qualified to 10 MW/m^2 at 2.5 M$/m^2.

4. A mix with dd_frac at or above Helion’s 0.49 carries its tritium-plant consequences, priced as an added fuel-handling cost of 50 M$ fixed plus 0.657 M$ per MW of plant neutron power: a unit-priced allowance rather than a costed scope.

5. The in-plasma tritium burn dhe3_f_T is floored by the per-pulse burnup, so a low-burn claim at high per-pulse burnup is flagged as requiring prompt triton loss. The floor rises as B^2 with the burnup: 0.16% at the patent basis, 0.31% at the 30 T extrapolation grant and 0.54% at the 40 T speculation grant, all inside the 1% tolerance, so every device’s zero passes. Section 5.2 derives the floor and prices the 40 T value rather than the zero.

6. The expansion-work recovery fraction is capped per point: f_pdv = min(claim, 1 - (V_i/V_f)^(2/3)), where the claim is the grade’s asserted f_pdv (the basis 0.80 through the extrapolation grade, 0.947 at speculation, f_pdv alone and not constraint 2’s composite, which at speculation is eta_dec 0.95 x 0.947 = 0.90), V_i the compressed flux volume at the point’s own bank and field, E_del/(B^2/2mu0), and V_f the chamber, the whole volume stroke counted (Section 6).

7. The shot yield is bounded by the plasma energy and the burn time, gain <= chi x burn time / breakeven time, with the breakeven time evaluated for the plasma at the burn point of the point’s own field and at the mix the point actually burns (purchased He-3 plus the bred He-3 that recycles), so every quoted point carries the burn time its shot implies, and the shot takes each device’s grade rather than serving as a shared basis (Section 6).

8. A shot’s implied burn must fit inside the pulse period: burn time x repetition rate <= 1, because one burn section holds one plasmoid and the next shot cannot fire while the last is still burning. The bound is hard at 1.0, reserving nothing for exhaust, refill and recharge.

5. The He-3 fuel cycle, the load-bearing subsystem

Tritium for a D-T reactor has to be bred because no one sells it at reactor scale. He-3 is in the same position: bought rather than bred, each of these devices would need 20 to 176 kg of He-3 a year, and no route supplies that. So breeding buys this plant out of the He-3 market, and what it buys into is a tritium inventory of tonnes: unlike a D-T blanket, which breeds and burns in one loop, this cycle holds its intermediate for a 17.8 yr mean life before it is fuel.

United States production is about 1 kg a year, nearly all of it from tritium decaying in the weapons stockpile [20]. Transmutation using an accelerator has a full design record, the DOE’s Accelerator Production of Tritium plant, a 1.7 GeV 100 mA linac drawing 170 MW of beam for 3 kg of tritium a year [21]; the speculation device’s 20 kg/yr would take seven of them and more power than the plant sells. Lunar extraction has sales agreements and no delivered He-3, and the world’s civilian heavy-water tritium stock, 25-30 kg, yields about 1.4 kg/yr as it decays.

The prices on record are all small-volume. The DOE Isotope Program’s 2011 allocation was 600 $/L to federal users and 1,000 $/L to commercial users [20], 4.5 and 7.4 M$/kg. The 2024-2026 transactions sit at 19-22 M$/kg on gram-to-kilogram volumes, four to five times that allocation: spot near $2,500/L per the Edelgas Group [22], Interlune’s agreements with the DOE Isotope Program and Maybell Quantum “at today’s commercial market price”. The 2 M$/kg this study charges sits below both, and the speculation entry, 0.5 M$/kg, nine times below the 2011 allocation.

The fuel cycle is the one in Helion’s patent [23], whose operating table also fixes the burn point: the patent’s mix (dd_frac 0.49) at 70 keV and 22 T radiates 23% of its fusion power as bremsstrahlung and releases 6% as neutrons. Slough [24], citing the same patent, restates that mix as the self-sustained cycle’s limit, He-3 at roughly 16% of the fuel.

The D-D reactions are what close the cycle. Half of them produce a He-3 atom directly, the prompt branch; the other half produce a triton, 0.5 dd_frac per primary event, a quarter of them at Helion’s mix and two fifths at the optimized devices. That triton is pumped out with the exhaust after the pulse, separated, and stored on getter beds until it decays into He-3 (12.3 yr half-life), and almost none of it burns before it is removed (dhe3_f_T = 0 in the basis). The plant therefore has no lithium anywhere, and its tritium plant is an exhaust-processing and storage plant.

The cycle rests on four conditions. The ladder prices three of them:

- the price of the He-3 it still buys. The optimized devices breed most of their own, so the fuel line is only 1.3 $/MWh at the extrapolation device and 0.2 at speculation.

- a site inventory of tritium held while it decays, tonnes of it at every device, whose plant and safety envelope are the allowance of constraint 4.

- per-pulse fuel recovery at four nines, the fourth of which is worth 4.8 $/MWh at the extrapolation device.

The fourth condition is not a price but a fact about the pulse: the store works only if bred tritium leaves the plasma before it burns. At Helion’s stated pulse it does. At the longer burn the granted shot needs, it does only if the plasmoid loses its tritons promptly, and every device rests on that loss as well as on the shot.

5.1 What breeding buys and what it costs

The He-3 released from the store replaces purchased He-3 atom for atom, building from empty as 1 - e^(-t/17.8 yr) and covering the 1 - dd_frac the plant burns only at equilibrium. This work levelizes the credit over the book life at the grade’s rate, as a scaling of the He-3 price outside the cost model, and the prompt bred He-3 counts as extra burns rather than supply.

A deuterium-rich plant therefore buys He-3 only until the store covers its need. Against the full bill, every atom it burns bought for the life of the plant, its levelized purchase is 22% at the records device, 16% at extrapolations and 11% at speculation, the share falling as the granted field rises, because more of the bred He-3 then burns per pass instead of leaking out of the recovery loop. A mix that breeds less than it burns keeps buying: Helion’s own device pays 79%. That exposure is what the mix is worth. At the ledger price the full bill would cost Helion’s device 5.2 $/MWh more and the records device 35.3, and free He-3 would save them 19.7 and 9.7, which would also change the mix (Section 4).

At the 2011 federal allocation, 600 $/L or 4.5 M$/kg, the extrapolation device reads 27.2 $/MWh against 52.3 for Helion’s mix in the same chamber at the same rate, and at the commercial spot 36.3 against 152.6. Breeding most of its He-3 makes it far less exposed than Helion’s mix, but not indifferent: the spot adds 10.6 $/MWh. A richer mix still, dd_frac 0.95, breeds nearly all it burns and reads 26.6 to 27.9 across the whole price span.

The store that breeding implies is large. Every module breeds tritium at half its D-D rate, 38 kg/yr across Helion’s plant and 53 to 145 at the optimized devices, and store-for-decay fixes the equilibrium inventory at that rate times the 17.8 yr mean life: 0.67 tonnes at Helion’s device, 2.6 at the records device, 1.3 at extrapolations and 0.9 at speculation. A tonne is 1e10 Ci, about 320 kW of continuous decay heat, 250 times ITER’s site authorization and 35 times the world’s civilian tritium stock. The inventory is intrinsic to breeding at this scale; shipping it to a central fuel facility relocates it, and can hold the reactor site near ITER-class inventory with weekly pickups, but cannot shrink it.

The store’s hardware is the allowance of constraint 4, and probably an underestimate: 50 M$ of fixed core, isotope separation at the firing rate with the safety envelope and the getter beds, plus 0.657 M$ per MW of plant neutron power, 180 M$ at the extrapolation device and 143 at speculation. Its licensing posture is carried by holding the site, decommissioning and O&M analogs at fission class rather than gas class, a refusal Section 7 prices at about 2.0 $/MWh.

A plant can buy its He-3 instead of breeding it, holding 80 kg rather than 900: the He-3-rich preset, which breeds only 4.6 kg/yr, reads 12.1 $/MWh at 1 GWe against the device’s 10.5. The smaller inventory costs 1.6 $/MWh.

The alternative to breeding in the power module is a dedicated deuterium breeder: a pure-D module of the same grade with its bred He-3 and tritium pumped out every pulse, priced per kilogram delivered as module ownership plus a throughput-sized tritium plant plus its net power at the device’s own LCOE (scripts/breeder_loop.py). It delivers at 2.0 M$/kg at the records grade, the ledger’s own price, at about 0.7 at extrapolations and 0.2 at speculation against the ledger’s 2 and 0.5, and at 6.6 at the Helion device, which therefore cannot self-supply at the price it is charged. The two cheaper grades move neither device outside its precision, 25.0 against 25.7 $/MWh and 10.4 against 10.5, and the breeder relocates the tritium inventory rather than shrinking it.

5.2 Fuel recovery and the burnup closure

Helion’s cycle depends on pumping the bred tritium out of the exhaust before it burns, so the store-and-decay idealization (dhe3_f_T = 0: no bred triton burns in-plasma) is a claim about pulse length, not a modeling choice. A triton born mid-pulse sits in a deuterium-rich plasma at fusion temperature for the rest of the burn, and D-T reactivity is 27-87x D-D at 70-20 keV [25], so each unit of D burnup burns 14-43x as much of the bred tritium. The per-pulse deuterium burnup pins the integrated exposure x_DD = -ln(1 - f_D) = n_D ⟨σv⟩_DD tau, and the thermalized-limit in-pulse tritium burn is

f_T = 1 - (1 - e^-x)/x,  x = (sv_DT / sv_DD) x_DD,

the fraction of a pulse’s bred tritium that fuses before the pulse ends and so never reaches the exhaust to be pumped. A triton born at the start of the burn sees the full exposure x and burns with probability 1-e^-x, one born at the end sees none, and the expression is the average over birth times; its only physics input is the reactivity ratio. Prompt orbit loss from the plasmoid, whose separatrix sits only a few ion gyroradii from the field null, is the one mechanism that can hold the burn below this floor; beam-target burn of the slowing triton can only add to it. This closure is constraint 5: any quoted point whose dhe3_f_T sits materially below the floor is flagged as coherent only under prompt triton loss.

The basis is the patent’s burn point, n_D = 0.75e22 m^-3 at 70 keV, at Helion’s stated pulse rather than the patent’s confinement time of 0.10 to 0.16 s: that time implies an n tau of 2-3e21 s/m^3, three to four orders above the n tau any FRC has reached on the uniform Lawson ledger [26], and is the reason the shot is graded rather than priced. Over a full confinement period a thermalized triton would burn 28-38%, the patent’s own case for inter-pulse removal.

Over a sub-millisecond pulse the burnup is 1.2-2.5e-4 and the floor on the in-pulse tritium burn 0.16-0.3%, inside the 1% tolerance constraint 5 allows: at the patent point, where the records and Helion devices sit, the burnup would have to triple before dhe3_f_T = 0 broke it. The floor scales with the density, so the granted fields raise it, to 0.54% at the speculation device’s 40 T, still half the tolerance.

The stated pulse is not the burn the shot needs. Constraint 7 puts the design-basis shot at a burn of 0.3 to 0.7 s, over which a thermalized triton burns 58 to 80% before removal; the speculation device’s 800 MJ shot needs 0.15 to 0.34 s, shorter because its 40 T plasma reaches breakeven 3.3 times faster, and over that a triton burns 70 to 87%. The store-and-decay cycle and the granted shot are coherent together only under prompt triton loss, the branch the burnup axis of Figure 5 prices at high burnup.

5.3 Neutron damage and the unshieldable throat

The replacement rate follows from the 14 MeV-equivalent neutron wall load and a dpa tolerance: damage accrues at 10 dpa per MW yr/m^2 in Fe-based structure, with the 2.45 MeV D-D flux folded in at 0.4. The tolerance is taken at 20 dpa, the edge of the irradiation database for reduced-activation ferritic-martensitic steel, and at 50 dpa, the DEMO-class design target. The event carries both the wall and the close-coupled coils (106 + 28 M$ in the speculation device’s single 30 m chamber, 211 + 61 across the extrapolation device’s four), since nothing 0.10 m behind the wall outlives it.

The devices are priced with no in-pulse tritium burn, so the spectrum is pure 2.45 MeV D-D and the 14 MeV-equivalent load follows the mix and the duty: 0.05 MW/m^2 at Helion’s device, 0.12 and 0.13 at the records and extrapolation devices, and 0.19 at the speculation device, whose one 30 m wall carries a plant’s neutrons at the deuterium-rich mix. That prices at 0.3 $/MWh or less at the 50 dpa design target, and 0.2 to 2.6 at the 20 dpa database edge.

These are the favorable end of the load. Section 5.2’s floor puts 0.16 to 0.54% of the bred tritium into the pulse, and a 14.1 MeV neutron displaces about 14 times as much as the D-D neutron it stands beside, so charging the floor rather than zero adds 2 to 8% to the dpa rate, on damage that accumulates for the life of the component.

Damage is small at these devices only because they sit at vanishing burnup with no in-pulse tritium burn, and the regime that would relieve the recovery requirement is the one that ends both conditions. At 15 to 30% of the deuterium burning per pulse a thermalized triton burns up to 90% of the bred tritium in-pulse, and the speculation device’s wall load rises to 1.14 MW/m^2, six times the 0.19 it is priced at.

Prompt loss and thermalized confinement then part by 10 to 21 $/MWh at the speculation device and 17 to 33 at the extrapolation device once wall replacement is counted, across the 50 and 20 dpa tolerances; on a divertor-class wall at 2.5 M$/m^2, the extrapolation stack in Helion’s chamber, replacement is prohibitive at +51 to +152 $/MWh. Keeping the tritium out of the burn is therefore cheap insurance at the devices’ own points and worth double digits off them.

Shielding cannot buy the machine out of this, because a pulsed inductive plant pays per pulse for every cubic meter between coil and plasma: it stores W = (B^2/2mu0) x annulus volume, and the circuit returns only eta_dec of that, 0.85 to 0.95 across the grades. A 0.4 m shield under the compression coil multiplies the 0.26 to 0.31 m^3 annulus by more than an order of magnitude, so the driver stores several hundred MJ a pulse and dissipates tens of MJ of it every shot: order 100 MW or more of recirculating loss, owed on a vacuum shot with no plasma in the chamber. The plasma side follows, since the larger delivered energy raises V_i in constraint 6’s cap on f_pdv. The B^2 makes all of it worse at the granted fields, 1.9 and 3.3 times the basis, so shielding is affordable only far from the burn, and the coils nearest it are sacrificial, ceramic-insulated, and on the wall-replacement schedule.

The audit also spreads the neutrons uniformly over the chamber while the burn sits near the compression section, so a localized audit would roughly double the damage costs, and at the speculation device nothing averages that away: one 30 m chamber carries the plant’s whole 1.79 GW.

The wall and coils are 134 M$ of the speculation device’s 645 M$ reactor account (272 of 1524 at the extrapolation device), so their levelized replacement outspends the account’s own capital service below an interval of about 5.7 years at the speculation grade’s 3% real cost of capital (2.8 at 7%) and 3.3 years at the extrapolation grade’s 5%. The derived intervals are well clear of it: 27 years at the speculation device and 39 at the extrapolation device at 50 dpa, 17 at the records device at the 20 dpa edge. The consumable-core regime, 1-3 year intervals with a fifth of the module’s value swapped near-annually, belongs to the high-burnup thermalized counterfactual, where the plant becomes a manufacturing operation with a waste stream this study does not price.

6. What a shot can return, and what the circuit recovers of it

Three of Helion’s public numbers cannot hold together: the shot Orion needs, the plasma conditions in the patent, and the stated millisecond pulse. At that pulse the fusion energy a shot returns is a fraction of what the circuit loses in firing it, and the shot needs a burn hundreds of times longer than Helion describes. That makes the disclosed design point incoherent, not the machine impossible: something Helion has not said publicly would have to close the gap, or the patent no longer describes the machine. The bounds below sit beyond the composite cap of constraint 2, which only stops the two conversion efficiencies from being counted twice.

6.1 The yield bound

The plasma at peak compression holds a share chi of the delivered driver energy E_del, the bank’s 50 MJ at the basis: merging shock and compression work are the only heating, and chi is fixed by the FRC equilibrium as a function of the separatrix-to-coil radius ratio alone, chi(x_s) = 1.5 β̄ x_s² / (1.5 β̄ x_s² + 1 - x_s²), with β̄ = 1 - x_s²/2 [27]: 0.30, 0.52 and 0.71 at x_s = 0.5, 0.7 and 0.85. That energy sits at the energy density of a beta = 1 plasma, 1.5 B^2/2mu0, and burns at the fusion power density of the burn point, so

gain <= chi x burn time / breakeven time,

where the breakeven time is the plasma’s energy density over its fusion power density: the time it needs to emit fusion energy equal to its own. It is the fusion breakeven time of Lackner et al. [19], from their comment on Helion’s own compression scaling [4]. That time is a burn, not a whole pulse, and a burn that lasts it returns chi E_del, so the gain against the driver is chi rather than 1. The pulsed gain of Nicolas et al. [18], fusion energy over plasma energy, is the same ratio against their Lawson time.

At the patent point (Section 5), at Bosch-Hale rates, the breakeven time is 86 ms with thermalized electrons, and 3.7 times shorter if the electrons could be held at a tenth of the ion temperature, which electron-ion equilibration at that density, about 1 ms, forbids over a burn this long [19]. Against Helion’s stated pulse of hundreds of microseconds to milliseconds [3] the bound reads two ways, and neither closes. A 1 ms burn returns 0.2 to 0.4 MJ across the x_s span, against the 2.5 MJ the circuit loses per shot even at eta_dec 0.95. The design-basis 128 MJ shot needs a burn of 0.3 to 0.7 s, three to seven hundred times the stated pulse; both scale inversely with Orion’s module count (Section 2).

6.2 What the field and the bank do to it

The bank and the field are scanned rather than pinned at Polaris’s values, as Kirtley’s response to this work recommends [28]. At beta = 1 the plasma pressure is the magnetic pressure, so at the patent’s ion temperature the density scales as B^2 and the breakeven time falls as B^-2: 1.9 times shorter at the 30 T extrapolation grant and 3.3 times at the 40 T speculation grant. The bank does nothing to the burn a shot of a given gain needs, gain / chi x breakeven time, so a larger bank does not shorten the burn; it fires a larger shot less often, and the duty falls as the bank grows.

Neither closes the gap. At the speculation device, with the granted field and four times the basis bank, the 800 MJ shot still needs a burn of 0.15 to 0.34 s, one to three hundred times the stated pulse: a higher field shortens the burn a given gain needs by the density ratio, and the larger gain the bigger shot represents lengthens it back. Any gain costs that many breakeven times over chi, at any field.

The bound reproduces the co-founder’s own D-T design. Slough’s 2 to 5 ms [24] is a transit, the passage of the FRC through a 3 to 6 m burn chamber, not a figure derived from his gain; the gain he quotes on the FRC energy, 9.1, implies a 3.4 ms burn under this bound, and the 20 to 40 MJ from a 3.5 MJ FRC in his abstract implies 2.1 to 4.3 ms across that bracket. Both sit inside the window. The check tests this arithmetic against an independently published design, not whether Helion’s own numbers hold.

This is constraint 7, and it is why the shot is a graded claim rather than a shared basis: every point the scripts evaluate carries the burn its shot implies, at x_s 0.7, at its own field, and at the mix it actually burns. Recycled He-3 counts in that mix, which at Helion’s device holds He3/D at 0.22 against the patent’s purchased 0.16 and gives a 0.32 s burn.

6.3 The duty bound

Constraint 8 requires the implied burn to fit inside the pulse period. Because the burn a shot needs is proportional to the shot, burn time x repetition rate is the module’s fusion power in disguise:

duty = burn time x rate = P_fus / (chi E_del / breakeven time),

whose denominator is the cap the table reads against. It is evaluated at x_s 0.7, where a coil-filling FRC holds half the driver energy, and the reading moves with chi across the span (the breakeven times are at the mix each point actually burns, recycled He-3 included):

All pass with room at the fat-FRC edge, x_s 0.85, and every optimized device would be excluded at x_s 0.5, where a thin FRC holds only 30% of the driver energy.

The cap is the fusion power one burn section can carry, not a statement about the shot alone. The bank raises it in proportion, and the field raises it through the breakeven time: 2.9 and 5.3 times at the two grants, steeper than the 1.9 and 3.3 of B^2 alone because each device also burns a richer recycled mix. Together they take one burn section from the records device’s 126 MW on the Polaris bank at 22 T to 2.7 GW, which is why one 30 m module carries the gigawatt at speculation and the wall, not the duty, is what stops it. Because the rate is a design choice, the bound excludes no mix; it sets the fewest modules each mix needs where it binds.

6.4 What the circuit recovers

The same chi bounds the recovery of the driver energy itself: the thermalized share can only come back through the expansion channel the fusion heat uses, at f_pdv, while the model returns all of it at the flat inductive efficiency. Closing that gap costs 1.3 to 3.1 $/MWh at the extrapolation device across the x_s span and 0.1 to 0.2 at the speculation device, whose 447 MW of circulating driver power sits against the extrapolation device’s 948 at a lower price per MWh; at the low-gain Helion device it is order 10 $/MWh. The reconciliation bands carry it.

Constraint 6’s cap, 1 - (V_i/V_f)^(2/3), never binds. The compressed flux volume grows with the bank and shrinks as B^-2, so it moves only from 0.26 m^3 at 50 MJ and 22 T to 0.28 at the extrapolation device’s 100 MJ and 30 T and 0.31 at the speculation device’s 200 MJ and 40 T, while the chamber it expands into is orders larger: the records device’s 10 m x 0.75 m chamber caps f_pdv at 0.940 and the extrapolation device’s 15 m x 1.5 m at 0.981, both above the 0.80 those grades claim; the speculation device’s 30 m x 1.5 m caps it at 0.987 against a claimed 0.947, whose composite 0.95 x 0.947 = 0.90 sits at its cap exactly. Geometry therefore permits what every grade claims; what the circuit actually returns is the open question, and the published views span 40% to 95%.

The large chamber therefore buys expansion ratio, not merely wall area: recovery cannot be improved without more chamber, and the field buys the same ratio in less of it, which is part of why the granted fields pay.

About a fifth of the expansion work is axial, against the end-field gradient [29]. Constraint 6 counts the whole volume stroke, as Helion’s own burn-cycle framework does, so the cap credits that fifth to the circuit; whether the end circuits return it is a design question, and a shot sequence on Polaris would measure it.

6.5 What the evidence says about the recovery

The cycle is Diesel-type with an Atkinson over-expansion (burn at roughly constant pressure, then expand further than the compression stroke), the framing of Helion’s own burn-cycle analysis [30], which quotes about 60% for a constant-pressure burn and above 90% for optimal cycles. Two published figures sit against it.

Nicolas et al. [18], whom Helion’s FAQ cites for recovery “exceeding 95%”, give that number only as an ideal Carnot-form bound and set it aside on the thin-cycle argument. Kirtley cites the same paper for up to 97.5% particle recovery, depending on the compression ratio [28]; this study has not checked that figure, and particle recovery is not the expansion-work share f_pdv prices.

Slough [24], the co-founder whose patent supplies the fuel cycle, derives 40% as the most an ejected FRC can return in a flux-conserving expansion chamber, where it comes to rest holding 1.5 pV of thermal energy against the field it has compressed, and states that an FRC does not free-expand. That is his own recovery topology, not the discharged compression coil this work’s f_pdv assumes, and Kirtley rejects the figure as a one-dimensional compression efficiency that neither describes Helion’s three-dimensional compression and expansion nor preserves adiabaticity [28]. It is carried as a risk row rather than a cap: at f_pdv 0.4 the extrapolation device reads 42.2 $/MWh (+16.5) and the speculation device 16.7 (+6.2).

Direct recovery is not a Carnot exemption: cycle form, the compression and expansion ratios, and the entropy of the deliberately irreversible merging set the efficiency, which is what the composite cap encodes.

7. What would reach $10/MWh, and what stands in the way

One device reaches the target, and only as a park. Two sweeps scale the speculation device’s own 1 GWe module: one holds the plant at 1 GWe while the modules shrink, the other shrinks the plant. Both keep gain and wall loading fixed and size driver and chamber to module power.

Module count at fixed plant size is nearly free: 10.5 $/MWh in the device’s single 1 GWe module, 11.1 in 5 of 250 MWe and 12.6 in 21 of 50 MWe, a spread of 2.1 across a twentyfold range in module size. Buildings, supplementary capital and balance of plant price plant totals, so the only modularity tax is per-module fixed items, biting below about 100 MWe.

Standalone plant size is expensive:

The standalone penalty is three things: about 141 M$ of fixed site buildings calibrated on fission-class analogs, the P^0.5 staffing law of the Idaho National Laboratory sodium-fast-reactor basis [31], and the absence of any fleet-learning term on equipment.

This concept’s economics therefore live in power parks, where the 50 MWe module of Helion’s gigafactory framing [5] is a manufacturing choice the model neither rewards nor punishes.

As a park the full speculation stack reads 8.2 $/MWh at 3 GWe, 1.8 under the target, against 10.5 at 1 GWe, 0.5 above it. Three things bring it there.

The first is financing: at the design-basis 7% real cost of capital the same machine reads 14.3 $/MWh (17.6 at 1 GWe), so the 3% real cost of capital, sovereign-class money with no mechanism behind it, is worth 6.1 $/MWh at the park, three times the margin under the target.

The second is the field. At 40 T, a leap no public source names, the park sits 1.2 $/MWh below the best design at the patent’s 22 T (9.4) and 0.4 below the best at the 30 T extrapolation grant (8.6). It still crosses at the basis field, so the target is reached on the coil the patent point already needs, and what the leap buys is margin, from 0.6 to 1.8 $/MWh.

The third is the bank, four times Polaris’s and worth 0.1 $/MWh at this field, within precision: with the bank at Polaris’s value the same stack reads 8.3 at 3 GWe (10.6 at 1 GWe).

The figure moves with the availability it is derived at, 8.8 $/MWh at the model default of 0.85 and 8.0 at a pinned 0.95. It grants capacitors at 0.1 $/J with a 1e10-shot life, the entry whose reversion to the 1e8-shot record costs 36.2 $/MWh at the extrapolation device; it carries the wall-and-coil replacement of Section 5.3, and runs a module with no margin left at its wall (Section 4). What would take the coherent park further, none of it in the evidence base at any grade:

- He-3 at zero cost. At the device’s own mix it is worth 0.1 $/MWh, within precision (10.3 at 1 GWe). The saving comes only if the design search is re-run at zero price, which switches the optimum to the He-3-rich preset of Section 4: 8.3 $/MWh at 1 GWe and 6.2 at the park, 2.0 under the device. The price is what picks the mix, since at the ledger’s 0.5 M$/kg the park breeds and at zero it buys.

- Gas-class site and decommissioning analogs. About 2.0 $/MWh at 1 GWe, all of it in the buildings and CAS50 lines, and not defensible for a machine carrying a tritium plant and 0.9 tonnes of tritium. The design that would make the case, the He-3-rich single module with no tritium plant and 80 kg stored, reads 12.1 $/MWh, so the analog alone would still leave it above the target.

- A real fleet-learning term on equipment, anchored beyond the fleet the nth-of-a-kind constants already assume, or it double counts. This study prices none, and it is the only mechanism that would reward the mass-produced small-unit strategy.

- A field one grade ahead. The scan prices 50 T beside the 40 T grant: the best coherent design there reads 10.2 $/MWh at 1 GWe and 7.9 at the park, within precision of the device at either size. The field’s worth has nearly run out at this grade, because the wall, not the burn, limits the module.

Behind every granted field, the basis included, stands the same open item: the compression coil. Its own field presses on it at 193 MPa at the patent’s 22 T and 637 MPa at the speculation device’s 40 T, from zero to full on every shot, about five billion times over an 80-year book life. No conductor or case in the public record has a fatigue basis at that stress and cycle count, and this work does not design the coil.

8. Conclusions

Four devices on one architecture read 112.1, 105.9, 25.7 and 10.5 $/MWh at 1 GWe by evidence grade. One stack reaches the target: the speculation park at 3 GWe reads 8.2 $/MWh, 1.8 under it, on a 3% real cost of capital worth 6.1, on a 40 T compression field worth 1.2 against the patent’s 22 T, and on a driver bank four times Polaris’s worth 0.1, within precision; at the patent’s field it reads 9.4 and still crosses.

The floor is carried by site infrastructure, operations and the capital of the module, not the driver bank. At the extrapolation device the capacitor cycle life is the largest lever at 36.2 $/MWh, ahead of the burn mix at 11.4, the firing rate at 9.2 and the cost of capital at 6.9.

The compression field and the driver bank scale with the plant, as Helion’s CEO advised [28], and the study grades both. The bank is a procurement: doubled at the extrapolation grade it is worth 0.3 $/MWh and quadrupled at speculation 0.1, because at the granted fields the wall, not the burn, limits the module. The field is the entry that moves the upper grades, taking the extrapolation device from six 10 m modules to four of 15 m and the speculation device from two 15 m modules to one of 30 m, worth 3.4 and 1.2 $/MWh, and moving the limit that binds from the burn to the panel wall’s steady flux.

Both grants are the generous reading, 30 T with no public roadmap and 40 T an explicit leap, each labeled so, and the coil that would deliver either is neither designed nor priced here: no conductor or case has been shown to survive billions of cycles at the stress its own field imposes, the basis field’s included.

Every optimized device burns at the deuterium-rich end of the mix grid, against the He-3-rich mix Helion publishes, and the reason is price rather than physics: at the prices charged here Helion’s mix sits 9.6 $/MWh behind the optimum at the extrapolation grade and 1.1 behind at speculation, and only at zero He-3 price does its half of the grid win.

The published design therefore presumes a He-3 supply the public record does not describe: an unpublished source, a separation path cheaper than the one priced here, or a cost of self-supply this work puts too high.

The same prices give an early plant a resale option. Each kilogram of He-3 it burns sells as 4.2 M$ of electricity at the speculation device’s own price and 7.1 M$ at the extrapolation device’s, but the kilogram itself sold for 4.5 M$ at the 2011 allocation and sells for 19-22 M$ today, so an early breeding plant earns more selling He-3 into the thin cryogenics and quantum market than burning it, until fleet supply floods that market.

Every number grants Helion’s gain, 128 MJ from a 50 MJ bank, which at the patent point needs a burn of 0.3 to 0.7 s against Helion’s stated pulse of a millisecond or less; the speculation device’s 800 MJ shot needs 0.15 to 0.34 s. Over burns that long most bred tritium fuses before it can be removed, so the fuel cycle and the shot hold together only under prompt triton loss.

What limits the module changes with the field. At 22 T the burn fills the pulse period, and the records device runs at 100% of the duty bound in 25 modules; at the granted fields the burn shortens as B^-2 and the panel wall binds instead, at 97 and 99.7% of its limit, so the rate ceilings granted above that are worth nothing.

Every device carries a tonne-class tritium store, from two thirds of a tonne at Helion’s own to 2.6 tonnes at the records device. The three above the records grade also rest on a per-pulse fuel recovery of four nines that no per-pulse measurement supports, worth 4.8 $/MWh at the extrapolation device and 47 at Helion’s.

Of the data Helion does not report (Section 2), the shot gates the whole plant. The recovery chain gates 2 to 17 $/MWh at the extrapolation grade, and the fuel-cycle data tens of $/MWh at every mix. The capacitor cost at cycle life, a supplier’s datum rather than Helion’s, gates tens more, and the reactor point’s compression field, with the coil that holds it, gates 3.4 $/MWh at the extrapolation grade and 1.2 at speculation, and whether any compression coil survives its cycle count.

The speculation stack is jointly heroic: 0.1 $/J capacitors with a 1e10-shot life contradict the derating physics that connects capacitor cost to life, the composite recovery sits at its theory optimum with zero margin, the 40 T field is a leap with no source and no coil, and the 800 MJ shot is a burn of 0.15 to 0.34 s, one to three hundred times Helion’s stated pulse.

Its floor is conditional on the field and on the recovery topology. The best design at the patent’s 22 T reads 11.6 $/MWh, and the device rebuilt at 10 T, with the burn it implies and the 17 modules that fit it, reads 34.6; Slough’s 40% recovery ceiling adds 6.2 $/MWh at speculation and 16.5 at extrapolations. The neutron-damage costs are probably underestimates, and the one conservatism, no thermal bottoming, would take 3.8 $/MWh off the extrapolation device.

The constructive reading: the floor, conditional on the gain, is low by any thermal-plant standard, and the architecture of factory modules, park deployment and swappable cores fits the failure modes the physics imposes. What would move the ladder from speculation to evidence is identifiable: Polaris can measure the shot and the recovery, while the capacitor’s and the compression coil’s cycle lives are qualification work beyond it.
